Executive view
- The July 28–29 FOMC meeting is the principal near-term catalyst for the dollar and U.S. rates. The Federal Reserve calendar confirms a press conference on July 29 and does not designate the July meeting as a Summary of Economic Projections meeting.
- EIA reported a 2.0 million-barrel increase in commercial crude inventories to 411.7 million barrels for the week ending July 17, 2026.
- The next EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report is scheduled for July 29, creating a defined event window for oil and related inflation-sensitive positioning.

Key drivers — verified facts
- Federal Reserve: The July 28–29 FOMC meeting is a two-day meeting with a press conference. The 2026 calendar marks March, June, September, and December—not July—as meetings associated with projection materials.
- Crude inventories: EIA reported commercial crude inventories excluding the Strategic Petroleum Reserve increased by 2.0 million barrels for the week ending July 17.
- Petroleum-data cadence: EIA lists July 29 as the next Weekly Petroleum Status Report release date and publishes the main Wednesday summary after 10:30 a.m. Eastern time under its standard schedule.

Conditional cross-asset interpretation
- A firmer-than-anticipated policy message could support front-end U.S. yields and the dollar, while a softer message could have the opposite effect. The direction and persistence of any move will depend on the statement, implementation note, press conference, and prevailing market positioning.
- The crude inventory increase may be consistent with softer oil-price pressure, all else equal, but the signal should be assessed alongside gasoline and distillate inventories, refinery utilization, imports, exports, production, and implied demand.

Risks and watchlist
- July 29 FOMC statement, implementation note, and press conference language, including guidance on inflation, employment, the policy rate, and the balance sheet.
- July 29 EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report, especially crude and product inventories, refinery inputs, imports, exports, and supplied-product measures.
- Any unexpected macroeconomic, geopolitical, or liquidity development that materially changes U.S. rate expectations or risk sentiment.
