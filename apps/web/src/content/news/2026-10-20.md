## What it is

A review of dated currency observations, selected short-term funding rates and nominal Treasury yields. The release date tells us when a document was issued; the observation date tells us which day its figures describe.

## Why it matters

Currencies and financing costs help explain how dollar conditions can reach other assets. The H.10 levels add dated currency context, while Treasury provides a verified comparison between October 16 and October 19. The CP and H.15 levels describe separate funding channels without supplying a verified daily change here.

Our USD interpretation is conditional: if U.S. yields rise relative to comparable overseas yields and other influences remain similar, that can support the dollar. These documents alone do not establish that relative move or a current dollar trend.

## What moves it

Currency values respond to relative policy expectations, growth, inflation and demand for liquidity. The Fed broad dollar index and EUR/USD have different composition and units; neither can be substituted for DXY without checking the relevant series.

Nominal Treasury yields combine expectations about future short-term rates, inflation compensation and term premiums. Their rise does not tell us which component changed. Commercial-paper categories and bank lending benchmarks also respond to different borrowers and financing conditions.

## Common mistake

Calling the October 16 observations an October 20 market price; treating the Fed broad index as DXY; using a single currency level to claim a current direction; equating effective federal funds with the target range; or treating the nominal ten-year yield as the TIPS real yield.

A basis point is 0.01 percentage point. The two-year change from 4.74% to 4.77% is +0.03 percentage point, or +3 basis points, rather than a 3% rise in the yield level.
