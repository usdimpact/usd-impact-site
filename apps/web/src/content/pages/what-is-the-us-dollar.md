The U.S. dollar comes first in the USD Impact framework because it often sits upstream of global pricing, funding, liquidity, and balance-sheet pressure. It does not explain every market move, but it is usually the right first filter before interpreting oil, gold, Bitcoin, equities, natural gas, or foreign exchange.

## The usual mistake: starting with the asset

Most market commentary begins with the visible price. Oil gets one story, gold another, Bitcoin another, and equities another. That approach can miss the common system beneath those charts.

Global markets clear through trade invoices, bank balance sheets, collateral, reserve portfolios, funding markets, hedging activity, and exchange rates. A move that looks asset-specific may therefore begin with a change in the dollar regime, real yields, liquidity, or funding stress.

The disciplined approach is:

1. Read the dollar regime.
2. Identify the likely transmission channel.
3. Test the asset-specific explanation.

The dollar is the first gate, not the entire answer.

## The dollar is more than domestic money

Inside the United States, the dollar is the national currency used for wages, taxes, prices, savings, and payments. In global markets, it performs several additional roles at the same time.

### Reserve currency

Central banks and institutions hold dollar assets as part of official and institutional reserves. Market depth and liquidity matter because reserve assets must remain usable during ordinary periods and periods of stress.

### Invoicing and settlement currency

A large share of international trade is priced, invoiced, or settled in dollars, including transactions where neither party is based in the United States. Firms can therefore need dollars because of the contract structure, not only because they trade directly with the U.S.

### Funding currency

Governments, banks, and companies around the world borrow in dollars. When their revenues are mainly in another currency, a stronger dollar or tighter dollar funding conditions can increase the local-currency burden of servicing those liabilities.

### Pricing reference

Oil, gold, and many other globally traded commodities use dollar-denominated benchmarks. The pricing currency can influence affordability and translation, although supply, demand, inventories, geopolitics, and market structure may still dominate the final price move.

### Foreign-exchange bridge

The dollar is commonly used as the crossing point between currencies in the foreign-exchange market. That market structure reinforces its role as the system's default bridge.

### Liquidity channel

During global stress, demand may rise for dollar cash, short-term dollar funding, and highly liquid dollar assets. In that setting, dollar strength can be a signal of liquidity demand and can also transmit tighter financial conditions to other markets.

## The dollar as infrastructure

Watching only the exchange rate means watching the visible price while missing the infrastructure underneath it.

A stronger dollar can:

- raise the local-currency cost of dollar-priced imports;
- increase the burden of dollar liabilities for some non-U.S. borrowers;
- change exporter and importer margins;
- alter hedging costs and capital flows;
- tighten global financial conditions when funding and liquidity are already constrained.

A weaker dollar can produce the opposite pressure, but the relationship is not automatic. The cause of the move matters. A dollar decline caused by easier liquidity is different from a dollar decline caused by deteriorating confidence, and assets may respond differently.

## Why 1971 did not end dollar centrality

The end of official dollar-to-gold convertibility in 1971 changed the legal anchor of the postwar monetary system. It did not erase the installed network of dollar use.

Dollar centrality persisted because reserve-currency status depends on more than a metal promise. It also depends on network effects, market depth, legal and institutional credibility, existing contracts, central-bank practice, global banking activity, and the difficulty of replacing an incumbent unit across an interconnected system.

Four reinforcing pillars remain especially important:

1. **Invoicing:** dollar-denominated trade contracts create transaction demand.
2. **Funding:** dollar liabilities create refinancing and hedging demand.
3. **Reserves:** official holdings support access to liquid dollar assets.
4. **Market depth:** broad participation lowers the cost of continuing to use the system.

Diversification away from the dollar can occur gradually without producing immediate replacement of the system at its core.

## USD is not the same thing as DXY

DXY is the ICE U.S. Dollar Index, a specific six-currency basket with a large euro weight. It is useful as one market measure, but it is not the full dollar system.

A disciplined dollar reading separates several questions:

- Is DXY rising or falling against its basket?
- Is a broader trade-weighted dollar index confirming the move?
- What is happening in the bilateral exchange rate relevant to the country, company, or asset being studied?
- Are real yields, credit conditions, funding markets, or liquidity measures confirming the same regime?

A DXY move can be meaningful without being sufficient. Basket composition can matter, and funding stress can appear before a headline currency index tells the full story.

## How the dollar reaches different assets

The dollar usually reaches assets through several channels rather than one universal correlation.

### Price translation

A change in the dollar can affect the affordability and translation of dollar-priced commodities and cross-border assets. This channel matters, but it does not override physical supply and demand.

### Balance-sheet pressure

Dollar liabilities become easier or harder to manage as exchange rates and financing conditions change. This can influence companies, banks, sovereign borrowers, and capital flows.

### Liquidity and risk appetite

A stronger dollar often appears alongside tighter global financial conditions, while a softer dollar can coincide with easier liquidity. These are tendencies, not fixed rules.

The asset-specific driver still matters:

- **Gold** can respond strongly to real yields, confidence, reserve demand, inflation concerns, and stress.
- **Oil** can be dominated by supply, inventories, spare capacity, geopolitics, demand, and logistics.
- **Bitcoin** can react to dollar liquidity, real yields, leverage, flows, regulation, and crypto-specific developments.
- **Natural gas and LNG** depend heavily on regional contracts, weather, storage, shipping, and infrastructure constraints.
- **Equities** differ by duration, leverage, sector, revenue geography, margins, and pricing power.
- **FX pairs** reflect relative conditions between two currencies, not the dollar in isolation.

## The dollar can be both signal and transmitter

The dollar may act as a **signal** of changing rates, policy expectations, growth divergence, risk aversion, or funding stress.

It may also act as a **transmitter** by changing debt-service burdens, commodity affordability, hedging costs, portfolio allocation, and financial conditions.

Separating those roles improves interpretation. A rising dollar may be reporting a stress event, transmitting tighter conditions, or doing both. The correct explanation depends on the driver and the confirming evidence.

## A five-step reading sequence

Use this sequence before making a cross-asset conclusion:

1. **Define the dollar signal.** Specify whether the evidence comes from DXY, a broad dollar index, a bilateral exchange rate, funding conditions, or several measures together.
2. **Identify the likely driver.** Consider real yields, monetary policy, growth divergence, inflation expectations, liquidity demand, or a local currency shock.
3. **Check confirmation.** Compare the move with real yields, credit spreads, volatility, liquidity indicators, or another dollar measure.
4. **Identify the asset channel.** Decide whether pricing, funding, opportunity cost, balance-sheet pressure, risk appetite, or a local supply-demand factor is most relevant.
5. **Use conditional language.** State what the evidence suggests and what could invalidate the interpretation.

This sequence reduces one-variable thinking and makes it easier to distinguish a broad regime change from an asset-specific event.

## What a dollar move does not prove

A stronger dollar does not prove that every commodity must fall. A weaker dollar does not prove that every risk asset must rise. A DXY move does not prove that the entire global dollar system changed in the same way.

The relationship is conditional because the driver, confirmation signals, and the asset's own fundamentals can change the outcome.

## Common interpretation errors

Avoid these recurring mistakes:

- treating DXY as the entire dollar system;
- assuming one fixed correlation applies in every regime;
- describing the direction of the dollar without identifying the cause;
- ignoring real yields, funding conditions, or liquidity confirmation;
- allowing a broad macro story to override an obvious asset-specific shock;
- turning a plausible transmission channel into a certain prediction.
