# IG-004 — What Changed First?

Class: EVERGREEN
Role: framework habit / diagnostic thinking
Target duration: 32–40 seconds
CTA: Learn the framework → Start Here

## Objective

Teach the viewer to sequence evidence before assigning causality: identify the first observable change, then test whether the move is dollar-led, real-rate-led, liquidity-led or asset-specific.

## Final voice-over

> A market move is not the same thing as its explanation. Before you attach a headline, ask: what changed first? Did the dollar move before the asset? Did real yields reprice? Did funding or liquidity conditions shift? Or did the move begin inside the asset itself? Sequence matters because the first visible pressure often tells you which transmission channel to test. Start with evidence. Then build the story.

## Scene plan

1. 0:00–0:04 — Hook: `WHAT CHANGED FIRST?` with four neutral timeline markers.
2. 0:04–0:10 — `MOVE ≠ EXPLANATION`; an asset tile moves while a headline remains blurred.
3. 0:10–0:18 — `CHECK THE SEQUENCE`; dollar → real rates → liquidity → asset-specific appears as a timeline, not as a fixed causal chain.
4. 0:18–0:29 — Four diagnostic questions appear one at a time: `Dollar first?` / `Real yields first?` / `Funding first?` / `Asset-specific first?`
5. 0:29–0:36 — `EVIDENCE FIRST. STORY SECOND.`
6. 0:36–0:40 — USD Impact end card + Start Here.

## Caption draft

A price move and its explanation are different things.

USD Impact starts by sequencing the evidence: dollar, real rates, liquidity/funding, then asset-specific forces. The objective is not to force a single cause. It is to test which channel moved first and which explanation is actually supported.

Educational only. Not investment advice, a recommendation, forecast, or trading signal.

## Source / QA gate

- Use only neutral diagrams; no current market direction.
- Do not say the first observable move always causes the next one.
- Preserve `sequence helps test causality` rather than `sequence proves causality`.

# IG-007 — Real Yields in 30 Seconds

Class: EVERGREEN
Role: glossary / One Concept
Target duration: 30–36 seconds
CTA: Learn → Real Yields

## Objective

Explain real yields as inflation-adjusted interest-rate pressure without implying that a single real-yield move mechanically determines another asset.

## Final voice-over

> A nominal Treasury yield tells you the return before inflation. A real yield adjusts for inflation compensation. In simple terms, real yields help show the inflation-adjusted return available from safer dollar assets. That matters because the opportunity cost of holding assets such as gold, and the discount rate applied to long-duration cash flows, can change with real yields. Important: real yields are one driver, not a complete market forecast.

## Scene plan

1. 0:00–0:04 — `REAL YIELDS IN 30 SECONDS`
2. 0:04–0:11 — Simple concept equation: `Nominal yield − inflation compensation ≈ real yield` with `conceptual decomposition` label.
3. 0:11–0:19 — `INFLATION-ADJUSTED RATE PRESSURE`; Treasury/TIPS motif.
4. 0:19–0:28 — Two neutral branches: `Opportunity cost` → gold; `Discount rate` → long-duration equities.
5. 0:28–0:34 — `ONE DRIVER. NOT A FORECAST.`
6. 0:34–0:36 — USD Impact end card.

## Caption draft

Real yields help frame inflation-adjusted rate pressure.

They can matter for gold, equities and other assets, but the relationship is conditional and interacts with the dollar, liquidity, risk demand and asset-specific forces.

Educational only.

## Source / QA gate

- Prefer Federal Reserve/FRED or Treasury/TIPS educational references for final factual evidence.
- Do not present the simplified decomposition as a precise pricing identity for every maturity/instrument.
- No current yield level is required.

# IG-008 — What Is Liquidity Stress?

Class: EVERGREEN
Role: funding/liquidity primer
Target duration: 38–46 seconds
CTA: Learn → Liquidity & Funding

## Objective

Separate liquidity/funding stress from the vague label “risk-off” and show a simple transmission path.

## Final voice-over

> Liquidity stress means financing or market-making capacity is becoming harder, more expensive or less available. That is different from simply saying markets are risk-off. Stress can appear through wider funding spreads, tighter collateral terms, weaker dealer intermediation or difficulty obtaining dollars. The transmission can move from funding conditions to balance sheets, then to market liquidity and asset pricing. The key question is not whether markets look nervous. It is where the financing pressure actually sits.

## Scene plan

1. 0:00–0:04 — `LIQUIDITY STRESS ≠ “RISK-OFF”`
2. 0:04–0:13 — Funding network with cash, collateral and balance-sheet nodes.
3. 0:13–0:24 — Four stress labels: `Funding spreads` / `Collateral terms` / `Dealer capacity` / `Dollar access`.
4. 0:24–0:36 — Transmission chain: `Funding → balance sheets → market liquidity → asset pricing`.
5. 0:36–0:43 — `WHERE IS THE FINANCING PRESSURE?`
6. 0:43–0:46 — USD Impact end card.

## Caption draft

“Risk-off” describes a mood. Liquidity stress describes a financing mechanism.

USD Impact separates the two by checking where funding, collateral, intermediation or dollar access is actually tightening.

Educational only.

## Source / QA gate

- Use conceptual, not live, spread graphics.
- Do not imply one funding indicator alone proves systemic stress.
- Keep “liquidity” and “solvency” distinct.

# IG-012 — Real Yields vs Gold

Class: EVERGREEN
Role: relationship / Macro Myth
Target duration: 36–44 seconds
CTA: Learn → Gold Drivers

## Objective

Explain why real yields can matter for gold while rejecting a mechanical inverse-rule framing.

## Final voice-over

> Real yields are an important gold driver because they change the inflation-adjusted return available from safer dollar assets. When real yields rise, the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset can increase. When they fall, that pressure can ease. But gold is not controlled by one variable. Dollar direction, risk demand, liquidity, central-bank demand, positioning and market structure can all matter. Use real yields as a driver check, not a guaranteed inverse rule.

## Scene plan

1. 0:00–0:04 — `REAL YIELDS vs GOLD`
2. 0:04–0:10 — `IMPORTANT DRIVER. NOT A LAW.`
3. 0:10–0:20 — Neutral opportunity-cost diagram: real yields ↔ relative attractiveness of non-yielding gold.
4. 0:20–0:33 — Gold-driver hub adds `Dollar`, `Risk demand`, `Liquidity`, `Central banks`, `Positioning`.
5. 0:33–0:41 — `DRIVER CHECK ≠ GUARANTEED INVERSE RULE`
6. 0:41–0:44 — End card.

## Caption draft

Real yields can change the opportunity cost of holding gold, which is why they belong in the first-pass driver check.

But gold can diverge from real yields when other forces dominate. Relationship does not mean certainty.

Educational only.

## Source / QA gate

- No generic “rates up = gold down” statement.
- Any historical chart later used must be dated and described as period-specific, not predictive.

# IG-013 — DXY vs EURUSD

Class: EVERGREEN
Role: FX structure explainer
Target duration: 34–42 seconds
CTA: Framework → Dollar Measures

## Objective

Explain why DXY and EURUSD are closely related while clarifying that neither represents the entire dollar system.

## Final voice-over

> DXY and EURUSD often look like mirror images for a structural reason: the euro has the largest weight in the DXY basket. So a large euro move can strongly influence DXY. But do not confuse that relationship with the whole dollar system. DXY is a fixed basket. EURUSD is one currency pair. Broad trade-weighted dollar measures, emerging-market FX and offshore dollar funding can behave differently. Use the relationship as context, not as a complete map of the dollar.

## Scene plan

1. 0:00–0:04 — `DXY vs EURUSD`
2. 0:04–0:12 — Two opposing neutral line motifs with label `closely related by construction`.
3. 0:12–0:20 — Basket graphic highlights euro as largest DXY component; exact weight omitted unless freshly verified at production time.

5. 0:33–0:39 — `ALSO CHECK: broad USD • EM FX • funding`
6. 0:39–0:42 — End card.

## Caption draft

DXY and EURUSD are closely connected, but neither is the entire dollar system.

USD Impact uses DXY as one signal and checks it against broader dollar, funding and cross-asset evidence.

Educational only.

## Source / QA gate

Role: global dollar-system primer
Target duration: 40–48 seconds
CTA: Learn → Dollar Funding

## Objective

Explain why entities outside the United States can need dollar funding and why that matters for global financial conditions.

## Final voice-over

> The dollar is not used only inside the United States. Banks, companies, investors and trade networks around the world borrow, lend, invoice and settle in dollars. That creates dollar funding needs outside the U.S. If obtaining those dollars becomes harder or more expensive, the pressure can move through FX swaps, bank balance sheets, credit and market liquidity. Dollar funding is one reason the global dollar system is larger than a single currency index.

# IG-020 — Why Borrow Dollars Through an FX Swap?

Class: EVERGREEN
Role: funding-mechanics explainer
Target duration: 42–50 seconds
CTA: Learn → FX Swaps

## Objective

Explain the basic funding purpose of an FX swap without turning it into a trading tutorial.

## Final voice-over

> An institution may hold one currency but need dollars for funding, settlement or asset financing. An FX swap can temporarily exchange one currency for dollars and reverse that exchange later at an agreed forward rate. Economically, it can transform a funding position from one currency into another without requiring a permanent spot-currency sale. The important macro question is the cost and availability of that dollar funding, especially when many institutions need dollars at the same time.

## Scene plan

1. 0:00–0:04 — `WHY USE AN FX SWAP FOR DOLLARS?`
2. 0:04–0:13 — Institution has Currency A; needs USD.
3. 0:13–0:24 — Step 1: exchange Currency A ↔ USD now.
4. 0:24–0:33 — Step 2: reverse exchange later at agreed forward terms.
5. 0:33–0:44 — `MACRO SIGNAL: COST + AVAILABILITY OF DOLLAR FUNDING`
6. 0:44–0:50 — End card.

## Caption draft

FX swaps are an important part of global dollar funding.

The macro signal is not the existence of the instrument. It is how costly and available dollar funding becomes when demand for dollars changes.

Educational only.

## Source / QA gate

- Use BIS/central-bank educational material for final reference support.
- Keep the explanation institutional and conceptual; no executable trade instructions, sizing, pricing tutorial or leverage guidance.

# IG-022 — What Is a Haircut?

Class: EVERGREEN
Role: secured-funding glossary
Target duration: 28–36 seconds
CTA: Learn → Repo & Collateral

## Objective

Explain collateral haircuts in secured financing and how rising haircuts can tighten financing conditions.

## Final voice-over

> In secured financing, the cash lender usually values collateral below its market value. That difference is the haircut. If one hundred dollars of collateral supports ninety-five dollars of cash, the haircut is five percent. A larger haircut means the borrower must provide more collateral value for the same amount of cash. So changing haircuts can tighten or loosen financing conditions even when the underlying collateral price has not changed much.

## Scene plan

1. 0:00–0:04 — `WHAT IS A HAIRCUT?`
2. 0:04–0:13 — Graphic: `$100 collateral → $95 cash`; label `5% haircut`.
3. 0:13–0:22 — Haircut expands visually; collateral requirement rises for same cash amount.
4. 0:22–0:31 — `BIGGER HAIRCUT → MORE COLLATERAL NEEDED`
5. 0:31–0:36 — `FINANCING TERMS CAN TIGHTEN WITHOUT A PRICE CRASH` + end card.

## Caption draft

A collateral haircut is a financing buffer, not a haircut to the asset itself.

When lenders require larger haircuts, borrowers need more collateral value to obtain the same cash amount. That can tighten funding conditions.

Educational only.

## Source / QA gate

- Arithmetic example is illustrative and must be labeled as such.
- Do not imply that every increase in haircut signals systemic stress.
- Keep repo/collateral terminology consistent with governed USD Impact funding material.
